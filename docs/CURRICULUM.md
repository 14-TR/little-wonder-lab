## Purpose and boundaries

An inviting field notebook for a child around seven: ask a question, make a prediction, try a fair comparison, notice evidence, and explain or redesign. The first three lessons are original activities, not copies of linked lesson plans. The sources support the science; their materials and age ranges are not automatically recommendations for this site.

This is an editorial curriculum, not an assessment system, a complete school course, or a claim about any particular child's development. Reading aloud, pointing, drawing, dictating, and simply watching are equally welcome. A grown-up may do the moving or folding while the child makes decisions. No task requires writing, timed reading, a correct first prediction, or buying materials. Each lesson can be explored on screen; the household investigation is optional. Screen exploration is a model, not evidence that a physical experiment occurred.

The suggested duration is **18, 18, and 20 minutes** respectively, including basic setup. Pausing, stopping early, or returning tomorrow is fine. Optional challenges and repeat bridge builds can take additional time.

Pattern Path adds an optional **15-minute** Math visit. This duration and its paper adaptation are editorial estimates, not trial findings.

## Scientific grounding and implementation notes

### Math branch: Pattern Path / `pattern-path`

Optional connection: `shadow-detective` → `pattern-path`, noticing what is present and predicting before checking. This is recapped within the lesson and never locks a route.

| Main question | Change deliberately | Keep the same | Compare | Reflection |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Which drawing comes next? | AB (leaf, flower) versus AAB (leaf, leaf, flower) | Same two drawn symbols; same order within each chosen unit | Identify each whole repeat, then add three drawings | Point, speak, dictate, or draw the unit and explain a choice |

This is comparison of deliberately arranged mathematical rules, not an empirical fair test. A fixed unit determines every drawing exactly; it does not measure plant growth or imply that all plants repeat. The science lessons retain their controlled-comparison requirements. Screen-only access needs no materials. Paper play uses a full sheet and a grown-up-checked large intact crayon, with no cutting, collecting or small-object activities.

NRICH's Pattern Making public-body excerpts support copying/continuing AB, progressing to longer repeats, continuing whole units, asking for the rule, and recording with invented symbols.[11] Developing Pattern Awareness defines repeated AB/ABC/ABB sequences and asks for the pattern rule.[12] Relevant retrieved excerpts were read; omitted sections were not. Our AAB botanical unit is an original adaptation, checked position by position in the model tests, not a source-attributed AAB activity. Neither source validates our duration, household adaptation or developmental outcomes.

The interaction offers fixed AB/AAB paths, five starting symbols and three additions, explanatory retries, explicit advance, restart and switching. Persistent native controls retain keyboard focus; sequence text and grouped drawings carry the same rule. No score, timer, stored answers or network activity is needed. Printed prediction copy supplies both unfinished paths independently of interactive state, together with all steps, safety and grown-up references.

### Shadows: light geometry, not a growing object

A shadow forms when an object blocks light on its way to a receiving surface. For a nearby spreading light source and a fixed wall, moving an opaque object toward the light enlarges its projected shadow.[1] The child-facing explanation deliberately says **in this setup** and **usually**.

Implementation requirements:

- Fix the illustrated wall and light. Move the blocking object along the light-to-wall direction, keeping its size and pose fixed. Make the shadow larger toward the light and smaller toward the wall.
- Treat drawn rays as a simplified straight-line model. Do not claim that a slider reproduces every lamp, computes calibrated measurements, or demonstrates all wave behavior.
- A finite-sized or multi-element lamp can make soft or overlapping edges; this is an implementation/experimental caveat, not a second lesson about blur. Do not make sharpness a guaranteed function of brightness.
- Do not extend the nearby-lamp size rule to a hand moving nearer the Sun. Outdoor shadow geometry is a separate future question.
- The physical option uses **an already safely set-up, cool, mains-powered LED lamp operated by a grown-up**. No batteries, electrical assembly, hot lamps, direct-sun activity, or laser is needed. If this lamp is unavailable or inconvenient, use the screen-only option rather than improvise a hazardous source.

### Ramps: a tendency with real experimental variation

Science World's ramp activity supports comparing steepness and surface effects.[3] The energy background explains that a higher starting position can supply more gravitational potential energy, which can become motion; friction and air resistance transfer energy to other forms rather than destroying it.[5]

Implementation requirements:

- The test compares **the same car** at two low ramp heights. With the ramp length fixed, changing height also changes angle. Do not present height and angle as independently controlled variables in this lesson.
- The observation is **roll-out stopping distance on the floor**, measured from the ramp's bottom to the car's stopped front wheels. It is not speed, elapsed time, or total path length.
- Greater height can be shown yielding more roll-out in a simplified fixed-surface model. Label this an **illustrated model**, not recorded trial data or an exact prediction for a child's floor.
- Avoid numerical meters/seconds, exact distances, coefficients, or timed-looking readouts unless an explicit validated mathematical model and its assumptions are provided. The initial lesson does not require one.
- Wheel bearings, rolling resistance, release technique, cardboard bending, and the ramp-to-floor join can affect results. The car can bounce or stop early; that is a setup clue, not a wrong child answer. Never suggest that gravity becomes stronger on a steeper household ramp.
- A single deterministic animation does not constitute repeated independent physical trials. Three runs of the real car may differ; the child compares groups without needing an average.

### Bridges: bending under a fixed load, not maximum strength

Changing a paper bridge's cross-sectional shape can make it harder to bend across its span without adding material; upright portions are the key feature of the channel design used here.[4]

Implementation requirements:

- Show a flat sheet and a U-shaped channel, not an arch. The **two folds run lengthwise from support to support**, and the walls point up. A transverse crease is a different design.
- Keep the paper amount, span, support overlap, and test load consistent. Use two fresh matching sheets: an unfolded creased sheet is not a clean flat-paper control.
- The load is **one unbroken adult-size plastic spoon**, placed across the bridge's width at midspan, unable to touch either book. Do not illustrate coins, small weights, or an arbitrary pile of loads.
- Use qualitative bending: nearly level, sagging, or fallen. A simplified drawing may show the folded paper bending less, but should not announce guaranteed success or a load capacity in grams.
- Stiffness is resistance to bending in the tested direction; it is not interchangeable with maximum breaking load. One spoon test cannot establish the strongest possible shape or a bridge's maximum capacity.
- A short gap or a light spoon may leave both bridges standing. That is a valid outcome. Do not tell the child to add hazardous weight to produce a dramatic collapse.

## Consistent lesson rhythm

1. **Invite:** one small question, an original illustration, a friendly purpose.
2. **Predict:** offer reasonable possibilities and ask for a reason; no points for guessing the expected outcome.
3. **Plan:** name the deliberate change and the important things to keep the same.
4. **Compare:** try both conditions; observe before revealing the explanation where the interface allows it.
5. **Check:** repeat a comparison where practical, or consider whether the result is distinguishable. Repetition is not a guarantee of certainty.
6. **Reflect:** “I thought… I noticed… We kept… Next I wonder…” Accept disagreement with the model when it describes the child's real observation.

A warm story may motivate a test, but the explanation must remain causal: shadows do not choose to grow; gravity does not reward a racer; folded paper does not become a different material. Praise care, noticing, revision, and kind collaboration rather than cleverness, speed, or winning.

## Safety and access policy

- A responsible grown-up checks the setting and materials. Safety text is available **before** hands-on steps, not only after the activity.
- No hot devices, heat sources, flames, lasers, loose or button/coin batteries, battery handling, magnets, coins, marbles, small detachable parts, glass, sharp tools, tasting, chemicals, climbing, or heavy loads in these initial lessons. The lessons do not ask the child to wire or plug in anything.
- Use stable, floor-level book supports; protect walkways and keep materials away from pets and younger children. Ramps carry toys; bridges carry a light spoon, never bodies.
- A caregiver's adult-sized hand is only a rough household length guide. No inference about the child's body, age, identity, or abilities should be recorded.
- Offer keyboard/button alternatives to dragging, reduced-motion or still-image states, readable contrast, and printable instructions. Do not make color, audio, dexterity, or animation the only way to understand a result.
- Source pages may recommend different materials, including small loads. Our safety adaptations take precedence; external links are grown-up reference reading, not instructions to follow wholesale.
